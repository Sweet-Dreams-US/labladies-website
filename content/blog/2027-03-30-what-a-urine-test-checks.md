A urine test is one of the most common lab tests. It is also one of the easiest to give. You only need to provide a sample. Many people still wonder what the lab does with it. This article explains, in plain words, what a lab looks at in a urine sample, why a provider may ask for the test, and what to know before you give a sample.

## What a urine test is

The medical name for a routine urine test is urinalysis. MedlinePlus, a service of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, describes it as the physical, chemical, and microscopic examination of urine. The goal is to detect and measure substances that pass out of the body in urine.

Your provider decides what kind of sample is needed. Two common kinds are a clean catch sample and a 24 hour collection. Giving the sample involves normal urination and causes no discomfort.

The lab then examines the sample in three ways. Each one is described below.

## Part one: how the urine looks

The first step is simply to look. The lab checks the color and the clarity. Is the urine clear or cloudy? Is it pale, dark yellow, or another color?

Normal urine can range from almost colorless to dark yellow. Food can also change the color. Beets and blackberries, for example, can turn urine red. A color change by itself is not a diagnosis, which is why the lab also tests the sample in other ways.

## Part two: the test strip

The second step uses a dipstick. This is a thin strip with small chemical pads. The strip is dipped in the sample, and each pad changes color if it reacts with the substance it was made to find.

The substances checked this way include glucose, ketones, protein, bilirubin, and blood.

:::note What is usually found
According to MedlinePlus, glucose, ketones, protein, and bilirubin are usually not detectable in urine. Hemoglobin, nitrites, red blood cells, and white blood cells are not normally found either.
:::

## Part three: the microscope

The third step is a look under a microscope. The lab searches the sample for cells, crystals, urinary casts, and mucus. It also checks for bacteria or other germs.

Taken together, the three parts give a fuller picture than any one of them alone.

## Why a provider may ask for the test

MedlinePlus lists several reasons for a urinalysis. A provider may order it as a routine screening for early signs of disease. It may be used to look for signs of diabetes or kidney disease, or to follow a person during treatment for those conditions. It is also used to check for blood in the urine and to diagnose a urinary tract infection.

Having a urine test does not mean something is wrong. It is a common part of routine care.

## Before you give a sample

Some medicines change the color of urine without any sign of disease. For that reason, your provider may ask you to stop taking medicines that could affect the results. MedlinePlus gives these examples: iron supplements, nitrofurantoin, phenazopyridine, riboflavin, and triamterene.

Do not stop any medicine on your own. Tell your provider about every medicine, supplement, and vitamin you take, and let your provider decide what to do before the test.

## Reading your results

Your results come back with reference ranges. A reference range is the span of results considered normal, based on large groups of healthy people. Ranges can differ slightly from one lab to another, so use the range printed on your own report. Results from different labs usually cannot be compared directly.

A result outside the range may or may not point to a health problem. A result inside the range does not guarantee good health. MedlinePlus says abnormal urine findings may be linked to conditions such as kidney disease, a urinary tract infection, kidney stones, or diabetes that is not well controlled. An abnormal result alone does not establish a diagnosis.

Your provider reads the result together with your exam, your health history, and your family history. If you can see your results online, still ask your provider what they mean for you. Even a normal result is worth discussing.

## Why one result is not the whole story

MedlinePlus notes that many things can affect lab results. These include food and drink, medicines and supplements, hard exercise, and not following preparation instructions. Tests can also produce false positives or false negatives. If your provider has doubts about a result, another test may be ordered. This is routine, and it does not mean something is wrong.

A result that is negative or normal means the test did not find what it was looking for. A positive or abnormal result means the test found it, and more testing may be needed to confirm. An inconclusive result is unclear, and a retest is likely.
