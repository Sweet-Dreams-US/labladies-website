When your doctor wants to learn more about your health, you may be asked for a lab test, a physical exam, an imaging test, or more than one. People often wonder why one visit leads to another, and what each step is for. This article explains what a lab test is, what it can show, and how your doctor uses it together with other information. It is general education only. Your own care is a matter for you and your doctor.

## What a lab test is

MedlinePlus, the health information site of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, explains that lab tests analyze a sample of blood, urine, or other body tissue or fluid. The sample is collected from you and then checked in a laboratory.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) uses the term in vitro diagnostics for tests done on samples such as blood or tissue. The FDA says these tests can be used to detect disease and other medical conditions, and to track overall health.

A blood test is one of the most common lab tests. MedlinePlus says blood tests look at cells, chemicals, proteins, or other substances in your blood.

## What a lab test can show

MedlinePlus says some lab tests confirm a specific condition, such as pregnancy. Others give a general overview of health. Lab tests are often part of routine checkups, which lets your doctor look for changes over time. They also help doctors diagnose conditions, plan or evaluate treatment, and monitor diseases.

MedlinePlus gives several examples of blood tests. A complete blood count looks at red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets and hemoglobin. A basic metabolic panel looks at glucose, calcium and electrolytes. Other blood tests look at cholesterol and triglycerides, or at how well blood clots.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus says the most common way to collect a blood sample is venipuncture, where blood is drawn from a vein, usually in the arm. A finger prick is another option.
:::

## How a lab test fits with an exam and imaging

A lab test studies a sample taken from your body. A physical exam and an imaging test are different ways of gathering information. Your doctor may use all of them.

MedlinePlus is clear that a lab result is read together with other information. Your provider interprets results alongside your physical exam, your health and family history, and imaging. This is the main reason a doctor may order more than one kind of test. No single piece of information is meant to answer every question.

For that reason, MedlinePlus also notes that blood results do not always tell the whole story. You may need other types of tests before a diagnosis is made.

## What a result does and does not mean

Each lab reports a range based on results from healthy groups. MedlinePlus says only the range printed on your own lab report applies to your results. Different labs can use different ranges and different units, so check the units on your report.

A result outside the range may or may not point to a problem. A result inside the range does not guarantee good health. MedlinePlus says false positives and false negatives can occur, though they are uncommon.

MedlinePlus explains the common words on a report. Negative means the test did not find what it was looking for. Positive means it did. Inconclusive means the result is unclear, and a retest may be needed.

## What can change a result

MedlinePlus says many things can affect lab results. These include food, drink, medicines, supplements, intense exercise, menstrual periods, and whether you followed preparation instructions such as fasting. Another MedlinePlus page adds that age, sex, race and diet can also play a part.

This is why the instructions you get before a test matter. If you were told to fast, or to hold off on something, follow what your provider told you. If you are not sure, ask before the test.

## Who makes sure labs follow rules

The FDA explains that a federal program called the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) oversees clinical laboratories in the United States. Labs are required to have the appropriate certificate before they can accept human samples for testing.

:::note Worth knowing
Under CLIA, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services issues certificates and conducts inspections. The FDA categorizes tests by complexity, and the CDC provides technical assistance and develops laboratory standards.
:::

## After the test

MedlinePlus says at-home test kits should not replace tests ordered by your provider. It also says to talk with your provider about all of your results, even the normal ones. A result is only useful when someone who knows your history looks at it.

If you have questions about a result, write them down before your appointment. Ask what the number means for you, whether the test should be repeated, and what comes next.

## The short version

A lab test looks at a sample of blood, urine, or other tissue or fluid. A physical exam and imaging give your doctor other kinds of information. Your doctor reads all of it together, and one result rarely settles a question alone.

This article is general information and not medical advice. Talk with your doctor about your own care.
