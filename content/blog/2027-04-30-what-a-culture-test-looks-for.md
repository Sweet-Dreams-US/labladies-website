If your doctor has ever said they want to "send off a culture," you may have wondered what that means. A culture test is one of the most common ways a lab checks for germs. This article explains what the test looks for, how the lab does it, and what the results can mean. It is general information only.

## What a culture test is

A bacteria culture test checks whether a person has a bacterial infection. If there is an infection, the test also helps find out which type of bacteria is causing it. According to MedlinePlus, a service of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, knowing the type of bacteria helps guide treatment.

The word "culture" here does not mean anything to do with customs or art. It means growing something in a lab. The lab gives germs a place and the right conditions to grow, so there are enough of them to see and identify.

## What kinds of samples are used

The sample depends on where an infection might be. MedlinePlus lists several common examples: throat swabs, urine, sputum (the phlegm coughed up from the lungs), stool, blood, and swabs or fluid taken from a wound.

Each of these has its own name. A throat culture is used to diagnose or rule out strep throat. A urine culture is used to diagnose urinary tract infections and identify the bacteria. A sputum culture helps diagnose respiratory infections such as pneumonia and tuberculosis. A blood culture looks for bacterial or fungal infections in the blood. A stool culture can find digestive infections, including food poisoning. A wound culture looks for infection in open wounds or burns.

## How the lab grows a sample

After the sample is collected, it goes to a laboratory. There, it is placed in a dish with a special substance that lets bacteria grow. MedlinePlus says enough bacteria usually grow in one to two days for testing, so results often come back within a few days. Some kinds of bacteria grow slowly and can take five days or longer.

If bacteria grow, the lab works to identify which kind they are. If nothing grows, that is also a result.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus says a urine culture takes the lab 24 to 48 hours to determine which bacteria or yeast, if any, are present. Timing for other cultures can differ.
:::

## How a urine sample is collected

A urine culture is one of the most common culture tests. The usual method is called a clean catch sample. It can be collected at a provider's office or at home with a special kit. In some cases, a provider or hospital staff member uses a thin tube called a catheter to collect urine from the bladder. In rare cases, a needle is used through the skin of the lower belly.

If you are able to plan ahead, MedlinePlus says to collect the sample when urine has been in the bladder for 2 to 3 hours. Ask the person who gives you the kit exactly how to collect it, because the steps are meant to keep the sample clean.

## What the results can mean

Results are usually described in simple terms. If harmful bacteria are found, MedlinePlus says this usually means a bacterial infection, and the test should identify the type of bacteria. A provider may order more tests to confirm a diagnosis or to learn how serious it is. The provider may also order an antibiotic sensitivity test, which checks which medicine will work best against the bacteria that grew.

If no bacterial infection is found, that is a normal result. For a urine culture, MedlinePlus calls this "normal growth," and says it means there is no infection. Normal ranges can vary a little between labs, so the report from your own lab is the one to ask about.

MedlinePlus also explains that test results in general can be negative, positive, or inconclusive. Negative means the test did not find what it was looking for. Positive means it did. Inconclusive means the result was not clear, and you will probably be tested again.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus says recent antibiotic use can cause a false-negative urine culture result. A false negative means the test missed bacteria that were there. Tell your provider about any medicine you have taken recently.
:::

## Why antibiotics are not always the answer

MedlinePlus points out that antibiotics only treat bacterial infections. It also notes that using them when they are not needed can lead to antibiotic resistance, which makes future infections harder to treat. This is one reason a culture result matters. It helps your provider see whether bacteria are involved before deciding what to do. Decisions about medicine are always up to you and your doctor.

## Questions you can ask your provider

You may find it useful to ask a few simple questions when a culture is ordered:

What is the test looking for? How should the sample be collected? How long will results take? Who will call me with the results? What happens next if the result is positive, and what happens if it is negative?

Writing the answers down, or asking a family member to listen with you, can make the next step easier to follow.
