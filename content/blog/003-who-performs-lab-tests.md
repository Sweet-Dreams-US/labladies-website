A single lab test passes through several sets of hands. One person orders it, another collects the sample, a team at the laboratory runs the test, and someone has to explain what the result means. Each person has a different job.

This article explains who does what. Knowing the roles makes it easier to know whom to ask when you have a question about a test or a result.

## The provider who orders the test

Most lab tests start with a doctor, nurse practitioner or other health care provider. They decide which tests are needed and write the order.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics notes that laboratory staff perform tests that physicians, surgeons or other health care practitioners order. MedlinePlus explains that providers order lab tests for several reasons: to help find or rule out a condition, to plan or check on treatment, and to keep track of a condition over time.

The provider who orders the test is also the person who receives the results. That makes them the main person to talk to afterward.

## The person who collects the sample

The next step is collecting the sample. For blood, this is usually done by drawing blood from a vein in the arm.

MedlinePlus says that during this kind of draw, a lab professional known as a phlebotomist takes a blood sample from a vein in your arm with a small needle. It usually takes less than five minutes. MedlinePlus also notes that some samples, such as blood taken from an artery, are often drawn by a specially trained provider.

:::note Worth knowing
The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics lists the main duties of a phlebotomist: draw blood, verify the patient's identity, label the collected blood for testing, and enter the sample information into a database. They also label containers for other samples, such as urine, and explain how to collect them.
:::

The person collecting the sample does not test it or read the result. Their job is to collect it correctly, label it correctly and send it on in good condition.

## The laboratory team

At the laboratory, the sample is tested by trained staff. The Bureau of Labor Statistics describes two main roles:

- **Clinical laboratory technologists**, also called medical laboratory scientists. They generally perform the more complex tests and procedures.
- **Clinical laboratory technicians**, also called medical laboratory technicians. They usually work under the general supervision of technologists or laboratory managers.

Both test and analyze samples such as blood, urine and tissue. They run equipment such as microscopes and automated cell counters, enter results into the patient's medical record, and discuss results with physicians when needed. Many specialize in areas such as blood banking, chemistry, blood cell testing or microbiology.

Some samples also go to a pathologist. The Bureau of Labor Statistics describes pathologists as doctors who study the cause and development of diseases. Lab staff prepare tissue samples for them to examine.

MedlinePlus sums up the lab's part simply: a technician or your doctor analyzes the samples to see whether your results fall within the normal range.

## Who checks the laboratories

Laboratories are checked too. In the United States, clinical labs work under a federal law called the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments, or CLIA.

:::note Worth knowing
The FDA says three federal agencies run the CLIA program. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services issues laboratory certificates and inspects labs. The FDA sorts tests by how complex they are. The CDC develops technical standards and laboratory practice guidelines.
:::

A lab must have the right CLIA certificate before it can accept human samples for testing. This is one way the system makes sure that the people and equipment running your test meet federal standards.

## Who explains the results

Your provider is the person who explains your results. MedlinePlus says it is important to talk with your provider to find out what your results mean for you.

This is because a lab result is only one piece of information. MedlinePlus explains that your provider uses your results along with your physical exam, your health history, your family history and any imaging tests. MedlinePlus also notes that different labs may use different normal ranges, and that it is common for healthy people to sometimes have a result outside the range.

The Cleveland Clinic adds that blood test results are not diagnoses. An abnormal result may not mean you have a serious medical condition. Your provider can explain what it means in your case.

## Seeing results in a patient portal

Your results may show up online before anyone calls you. The Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology, part of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, explains that since April 2021, a patient may be able to see test results at the same time they become available to the ordering clinician.

That means you may read a result before your provider has had time to review it. MedlinePlus says that even if you can see your results in your electronic health record, you should still talk with your provider about what they mean. If a result worries you, call the office that ordered the test.

## The short version

Your provider orders the test. A phlebotomist, nurse or other trained worker collects and labels the sample. Technologists and technicians at a certified laboratory run the test, and some samples are reviewed by a pathologist. The results go back to your provider, who is the right person to explain what they mean for you.
