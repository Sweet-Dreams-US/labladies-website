When you give a blood or urine sample, you trust that the result your doctor gets back is correct. That trust is backed by federal rules. Every lab that tests samples from people for health reasons has to follow them.

This article explains those rules in plain words. It covers the law behind them, the three federal agencies that share the work, and what it all means for you as a patient or caregiver.

## The law behind lab testing

The main federal law for clinical labs is the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments, known as CLIA. Congress passed it in 1988, so you may also see it called CLIA '88.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, or CMS, says the goal of CLIA is to ensure quality laboratory testing. CMS says the law set quality standards for all testing labs so that results are accurate, reliable and timely. According to CMS, CLIA covers about 320,000 laboratory entities.

The CDC notes that the detailed rules were published in 1992 and phased in through 1994. They have been amended several times since then.

:::note Worth knowing
The FDA says labs must have the right CLIA certificate before they can accept human samples for testing.
:::

## Which labs the rules cover

The CDC says CLIA applies to all U.S. facilities or sites that test human specimens for health assessment or to diagnose, prevent or treat disease.

There are a few exceptions. The CDC lists forensic testing, some research testing where results are not reported for individual patients, and certain employment drug testing done by labs certified under a separate federal program.

CMS also explains that facilities that only collect, prepare or mail specimens are not considered laboratories under CLIA. The lab rules apply to the place where the test itself is performed.

## Three agencies share the work

The FDA explains that three federal agencies run the CLIA program together. Each one has a different job.

CMS handles most of the day to day oversight. According to the FDA, CMS:

- Issues lab certificates and collects fees.
- Inspects labs and enforces the rules.
- Approves private accreditation organizations to perform inspections.
- Monitors how labs do on proficiency testing and approves the programs that run it.
- Publishes the CLIA rules and guidance.

CMS adds that state survey agencies are also authorized to set and enforce CLIA standards.

The FDA sorts tests by how complex they are. It also reviews requests from test makers who want a test placed in the simplest group, called waived tests.

The CDC works on the science side. It does research and gives technical help. It writes lab standards and practice guidelines, studies ways to improve lab quality, and manages an advisory committee that gives scientific and technical advice to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services.

:::note Worth knowing
The CDC says it does not issue CLIA certificates or process fee payments. That work belongs to CMS.
:::

## Simple tests and complex tests

Not every test is held to the same rules. The FDA explains that CLIA sets up three groups of tests:

- Waived tests
- Moderate complexity tests
- High complexity tests

The CDC describes waived tests as simple tests with a low risk for an incorrect result. Tests cleared by the FDA for home use are in this group. A site that runs only waived tests still needs a CLIA certificate and must follow the maker's instructions, but most other CLIA requirements do not apply.

Moderate and high complexity tests are together called nonwaived tests. CMS says the more complicated the test, the more stringent the requirements. The CDC says sites that run nonwaived tests must be inspected and must meet quality standards. The FDA lists what those standards cover, including staff, proficiency testing, quality control, quality assurance and inspections.

:::note Worth knowing
The CDC says that when a lab creates its own test, or changes a maker's instructions for a test, that test is treated as high complexity by default.
:::

## How labs show their results are right

One of the main checks under CLIA is proficiency testing, often called PT. CMS describes PT as the process used to evaluate a lab's performance.

Here is how CMS explains it. An approved PT program sends samples to the lab on a regular basis, usually three times a year. The lab must test those samples the same way it tests patient samples, with the same staff who normally do the work. The lab reports its results to the program. The program grades the results against CLIA criteria and sends the lab a score. CMS and accreditation organizations keep track of those scores.

Inspections are another check. CMS says some labs choose to be accredited by an organization approved by the Department of Health and Human Services, and that these organizations may have more stringent requirements. CMS also says labs must be certified for each specialty or subspecialty they test in.

## The FDA also reviews the test products

Apart from CLIA, the FDA regulates the test products themselves. The FDA calls these in vitro diagnostics, or IVDs. They are tests done on samples such as blood or tissue taken from the body.

The FDA says IVDs are medical devices under federal law. Like other medical devices, they are subject to controls before and after they go on the market. Test makers apply for a CLIA complexity group as part of the review before a test is sold.

So there are two layers. The FDA looks at the test product. The CLIA rules look at the lab that runs the test and how it runs it.

## What this means for you

You do not need to know these rules to get a lab test. A few points can still help.

- The lab that tests your sample has to hold a CLIA certificate.
- MedlinePlus says test results are usually accurate, but no test is perfect.
- MedlinePlus says labs often use different reference ranges for normal results, and that you can't compare results from different labs. If you have tests done at more than one lab, ask your doctor how to read them together.
- MedlinePlus also says at-home tests should never replace testing that your provider orders.

The doctor or other provider who ordered your test is the best person to explain your results.
