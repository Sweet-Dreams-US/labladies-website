If your doctor has ordered a PCR test, you may wonder what the lab is actually looking for. The short answer is simple. A PCR test looks for a very small amount of genetic material from a germ in a sample. This article explains what that means, how the test works, and what a result can and cannot tell you.

## What PCR stands for

PCR stands for polymerase chain reaction. The name sounds technical, but the idea is easy to follow. The National Human Genome Research Institute describes PCR as a laboratory technique that makes millions to billions of copies of one specific piece of DNA, so that piece can be studied in more detail.

Think of it as a way to make a tiny amount of something large enough to see. A lab cannot easily detect a few pieces of genetic material in a sample. After copying, there are enough pieces to detect.

## What the test looks for

According to MedlinePlus, PCR tests check for small amounts of genetic material from a pathogen, which is a disease-causing organism, or from abnormal cells. The sample can be blood, saliva, mucus, or tissue.

Genetic material comes in two forms. DNA carries the genetic information a living thing needs to develop and grow. RNA carries information copied from DNA. MedlinePlus notes that most viruses and other pathogens contain DNA or RNA, and some viruses use RNA instead of DNA.

So a PCR test does not look for symptoms, and it does not look for your body's reaction to a germ. It looks for the germ's own genetic material.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus states that PCR tests can find signs of disease in the earliest stages of infection, when there is only a very small amount of a pathogen in the body.
:::

## How the test works in the lab

After a sample is collected, the lab puts it in a machine called a thermal cycler, along with an enzyme called polymerase and certain chemicals. MedlinePlus explains that the machine heats and cools the sample in repeated cycles, and each cycle makes more copies of a chosen segment of DNA. After about an hour, billions of copies have been made. If a virus or other pathogen is present, the machine shows it.

The National Human Genome Research Institute explains that short pieces of synthetic DNA, called primers, choose which segment is copied. This is why a PCR test can be built to look for one particular germ and ignore everything else in the sample.

Some viruses, including the one that causes COVID-19, are made of RNA. MedlinePlus explains that for these viruses the RNA is first changed into DNA before copying. That version is called reverse transcription PCR, and you may see it written as rtPCR on a lab form.

## How the sample is collected

You do not need any special preparation for a PCR test, according to MedlinePlus. The collection method depends on the test. A blood sample is taken from a vein in the arm with a small needle. MedlinePlus says the draw usually takes less than five minutes and you may feel a small sting. A nasal swab may tickle the throat or cause some coughing, and those effects are temporary.

Other samples, such as urine, wound, or stool specimens, are collected in the way the performing laboratory requires. Your doctor or the person collecting the sample will tell you what to expect for your test.

## What a PCR result can and cannot tell you

A PCR result is one piece of information. Your doctor looks at it together with your symptoms, your history and other tests.

MedlinePlus points out two things that are easy to miss. First, because PCR tests are so sensitive, they can find pieces of a virus after you are no longer infected. A positive result does not always mean an active infection, and your doctor can explain what yours means. Second, other kinds of tests can miss early infections because there are not enough germs in the sample yet, or because the body has not had time to make antibodies. Antibodies are proteins your immune system makes to fight germs, and some tests look for those instead of the germ itself.

A PCR test is not a diagnosis by itself, and this article cannot tell you what any result means for you. Ask your doctor to go over the result with you.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus lists the main uses of PCR tests as diagnosing certain infectious diseases, identifying certain genetic changes that can cause disease, and finding small amounts of certain cancer cells that other tests might miss.
:::

## Who performs the test

The collection and the testing are two separate steps. The person who collects your sample does not run the PCR machine. The sample goes to a clinical laboratory.

The FDA explains that under the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments, known as CLIA, a clinical laboratory must have the appropriate certificate before it can accept human samples for testing. Ask your doctor which laboratory will test your sample if you want to know.

## Questions to ask your doctor

Before a PCR test, it helps to know what your doctor is looking for and why. You can ask which germ or condition the test checks for, how long the result usually takes, and how the result will be shared with you. After the test, you can ask what the result means for your own care and whether anything else needs to be done.

If you are not sure how to prepare for any lab test, MedlinePlus also has plain guides on blood testing and on understanding lab results.
