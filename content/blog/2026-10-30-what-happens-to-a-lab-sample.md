When a sample of your blood or urine is collected, the visit is over in a few minutes. For the sample, the work is only starting. It still has to be labeled, carried to a laboratory, tested and turned into a report for your doctor.

This article follows a sample through each of those steps. Knowing the path can make it easier to understand why labels matter, why results are not ready right away, and who you should call with questions.

## It starts with the order

Most lab tests begin with an order from a doctor or another provider. The order says which tests are needed. That decides which tubes or containers are used and how many.

The Cleveland Clinic notes that a phlebotomist may collect just one vial or several, depending on which tests your provider ordered. So one visit can cover more than one test.

## Checking who you are and labeling the sample

Before any sample is taken, the person collecting it checks who you are. The Cleveland Clinic says the phlebotomist will introduce themselves and verify your identity. You may be asked to say your full name and date of birth, even if the person already knows you.

Right after the sample is collected, each tube or container is labeled. The label ties the sample to you and to your doctor's order.

:::note Worth knowing
The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics explains that because all blood looks the same, phlebotomists must carefully identify and label the blood they collect and enter the information into a database.
:::

This is the most important step in the whole path. Once a tube leaves the room, the label is the only thing that says whose sample it is. That is why the questions at the start of a visit can feel repetitive. They are there to protect you.

## Getting the sample to the laboratory

After it is labeled, the sample is kept safe until it can be sent on. The Cleveland Clinic describes phlebotomists keeping track of blood samples and storing them safely until they are sent to the lab. The Bureau of Labor Statistics notes that some phlebotomists also ship or transport blood and other samples to different locations.

For a blood draw, MedlinePlus explains that the blood is collected into a test tube or vial. The Cleveland Clinic adds that the tube is sealed and sent to a lab for analysis.

## What happens inside the lab

At the laboratory, trained staff test the sample. MedlinePlus says the lab checks whether the sample contains certain substances and, if so, how much.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics describes the work of clinical laboratory technologists and technicians. They:

- Test and analyze body fluids such as blood and urine, and tissue samples.
- Operate lab equipment such as microscopes and automated cell counters.
- Use automated equipment that analyzes many samples at the same time.
- Record the results and enter them into the patient's medical record.
- Discuss results with physicians when needed.

The same source notes that lab staff set up, calibrate and maintain their equipment. This is part of how a lab keeps its results accurate from one day to the next.

## The rules labs follow

In the United States, clinical laboratories work under a federal law called the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments, or CLIA. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says CLIA sets quality standards for lab testing done on human samples such as blood, body fluid and tissue.

:::note Worth knowing
According to the FDA, clinical laboratories must have the appropriate CLIA certificate before they can accept human samples for testing. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services issues the certificates and inspects laboratories.
:::

Three federal agencies share the CLIA program: the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, the Food and Drug Administration and the CDC. Each has its own role, from inspecting labs to setting technical standards.

## Results go back to your provider

When testing is done, the lab sends the results to the doctor or provider who ordered the test. Your provider then looks at the results together with everything else they know about you.

MedlinePlus says providers use lab results along with your physical exam, your health history, your family history and any imaging tests. A result on its own does not give a full picture. MedlinePlus also notes that even if you can see your results in your electronic health record, it is still important to talk with your provider about what they mean for you.

The Cleveland Clinic says some health care organizations offer online access to results, but your provider may prefer to discuss them by phone or in person. Your provider will likely tell you how you will receive them.

## How long it takes

There is no single answer. The Cleveland Clinic says the time depends on the test and on your provider. Some blood test results are ready within a few hours. Others, such as genetic tests, usually take longer.

If you are waiting on results, the best person to ask is the office that ordered the test. They can tell you when the results are expected and how they will reach you.

## The short version

A sample's path has a few clear steps. The person collecting it checks who you are and labels each tube. The sample is stored safely and sent to a certified laboratory. Trained lab staff test it, and the results go back to the provider who ordered the test. Your provider is the right person to explain what the results mean for you.
