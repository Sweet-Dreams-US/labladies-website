A blood test starts with a small sample of your blood. A laboratory checks that sample for certain things and reports the results as numbers. Your doctor then reads those numbers along with the rest of what they know about you.

Many people feel nervous about blood work because the words on the report are unfamiliar. This article explains in plain language what a blood test looks at, what a few common tests are called, and how to think about your results. It is general information. Your own doctor is the person who can tell you what your results mean for you.

## What a blood test looks at

According to MedlinePlus, a blood test checks the cells, chemicals, proteins, or other substances in your blood. Different tests look at different things. One test may count the cells in your blood. Another may measure a chemical such as sugar. A third may look at a protein.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus says most blood tests need no special preparation. Some do require you to go without food and drink for several hours first. Your provider will tell you if yours does.
:::

## The blood cell count

One of the most common tests is the complete blood count, often called a CBC. MedlinePlus describes it as a test that measures red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and hemoglobin. Hemoglobin is the substance in red blood cells that carries oxygen.

A CBC is a count. It tells your provider how many of each kind of cell are in the sample. It does not by itself give a diagnosis. Your provider reads it with your symptoms, your history, and sometimes other tests.

## Sugar, minerals, and salts

Another common test is the basic metabolic panel. MedlinePlus says it measures chemicals in your blood such as glucose, calcium, and electrolytes. Glucose is blood sugar. Electrolytes are minerals in your blood that carry an electric charge.

If you see the letters BMP on a report, this is the test being described.

## Fats, enzymes, and clotting

Blood tests can look at other things too. MedlinePlus lists several groups of tests:

Lipid tests include cholesterol tests and a triglyceride test. They are sometimes called heart disease tests.

Blood enzyme tests include troponin and creatine kinase. MedlinePlus says these can help show whether someone has had a heart attack or heart muscle damage.

A coagulation panel checks for disorders that cause too much bleeding or too much clotting.

You do not need to remember these names. If your doctor orders one, ask what it is for and how to get ready.

## Why a doctor orders blood work

MedlinePlus says blood tests are often part of a routine checkup. Providers also use them to help find a condition, to keep track of a long-term condition, and to see whether a treatment is working. They can also check how well organs such as the liver, kidneys, heart, and thyroid are working, and how well your immune system fights infection.

MedlinePlus also notes that results are read together with your symptoms, medical history, and other tests, and that blood work alone may not give a full diagnosis. That is why a single number is rarely the whole story.

## Getting ready for a blood draw

Some preparation is simple. Tell your provider about all the medicines you take, including over-the-counter medicines and supplements. MedlinePlus says not to stop taking any medicine unless your provider tells you to.

MedlinePlus lists several things that can affect results: certain foods and drinks, certain medicines or supplements, hard exercise before the test, and whether you followed preparation instructions such as fasting. If you are not sure how to prepare, ask the office that ordered the test before the day of your draw.

The draw itself carries very little risk. MedlinePlus says a needle stick in the arm may cause slight pain or a small bruise, which usually goes away quickly.

## Reading your results

Every lab report lists a reference range next to each result. MedlinePlus explains that a reference range is the set of values considered usual, based on results from large groups of healthy people. Ranges can differ for children and adults.

Three points from MedlinePlus are worth keeping in mind.

First, labs can use different methods and different ranges. Compare your number only with the range printed on your own report, and use the same lab when you want to follow a trend over time.

Second, a result outside the range may or may not point to a health problem. Healthy people sometimes fall outside the range, and your provider looks at your whole health picture to explain it.

Third, a result inside the range is not a promise of good health. Someone with symptoms may still need more testing.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus suggests asking your provider what your results mean for you, whether any result is outside the range and what might explain it, and whether you need a repeat or additional test.
:::

## Questions to bring to your appointment

Results are easier to talk about when you come with questions. You might ask what each test was looking for, whether you prepared the right way, and whether your medicines or supplements could have changed a result. If you used an at-home test, MedlinePlus says to talk with your provider about the results even when they look normal.
